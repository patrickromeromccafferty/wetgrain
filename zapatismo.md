## Who Does Zapatismo Speak to Today?<br><br>

This article was originally published in Revista de la Universidad de Mexico in
December 2023.<br><br>

*Founded in 1983, the EZLN (Ejército Zapatista de Liberación Nacional) is a militant,
left-wing political organisation for the defence of indigenous rights and territory in
Chiapas, Mexico. In 1994, the EZLN (known informally as the Zapatistas) declared
war on the Mexican state in response to the implementation of the North American
Free Trade Agreement. This led to the San Andrés Accords of 1996, committing the
national government to uphold the right to self-determination of Mexico’s indigenous
communities. Politically, the Zapatistas remain an active, de-centralised force
concerned with governance in Chiapas and dialogue between Mexico’s marginalised
communities. Named after Emiliano Zapata, figurehead of the Mexican Revolution
of 1910, their cultural significance is also wide-reaching, their liberation struggle being
representative of a wider anti-neoliberal and anti-imperial resistance movement.*<br><br>

For Celso Cruz Martínez, a flower in the desert<br>
For Iván Gil. Tyoskujuyëp, amuum tu’uk joojt<br><br>

Whenever I have the chance, I ask people: where were you when you first heard
of the Zapatista Army of National Liberation (Ejército Zapatista de Liberación
Nacional – EZLN)? What were your first impressions? I listen to the testimonies
and chronicles of people from very diverse contexts and origins involved in the
protests against the Mexican government’s violent and repressive response to the
uprising in the early months of 1994. They tell me about the marches and the
initial speculation as to what kind of guerrilla it was (was it more like the FARC
or M-19?), the frenzied chants that sought to impede the violent annihilation of
a movement unthinkable in an environment celebrating the country’s supposed
admission to the first world through the signing of the North American Free
Trade Agreement with the United States and Canada. Some say the uprising gave
new impetus to the causes they were already fighting for; others remember how
it became particularly relevant that they belonged to an Indigenous community;
others still, spoke of their visits to Zapatista controlled territory, of their impressions
as first hand witnesses to the round tables and negotiations that led to the signing
of the San Andrés Accords. There are those who recall their active participation
in the Marcha del Color de la Tierra – the Zapatista Caravan – or share anecdotes
about when people from different Indigenous communities were for the first
time invited to speak before Mexican Congress; they recount how the two main
television channels, in the context of that historic march, organised a concert called
‘Unidos por la Paz’ (United for Peace) at which Maná and Jaguares played, but then
refused to broadcast the arrival of the Zapatista contingent and their companions to
Mexico City’s Zócalo; they remember the doubts expressed by Jaguares vocalist Saúl
Hernández himself, in the press conference preceding the concert, after he sensed
the event might be turned into ‘a trap and a way of questioning the Zapatistas’ visit’.
Others have quieter tales of their participation in Zapatista educational projects;
some speak of the disillusionment they felt with the Zapatistas years later when
they failed to back Andrés Manuel López Obrador’s candidacies; and others still
described their participation in the Encuentros Internacionales de las Mujeres que
Luchan (International Meetings of Women Activists), organised by the Zapatistas.
I think of my own story. As a Mixe woman, an Indigenous teenager, in 1994
my relationship to the movement in its infancy was tangential. I first heard of
the EZLN from one of my teachers, Mr Celso, who showed us newspapers and
magazines he’d bought in the city of Oaxaca. I especially remember the covers of
Proceso magazine and the first pages of *La Jornada*. We didn’t fully understand
what was happening, but we read and discussed the questions our teacher skilfully
put to us about the EZLN and our own position as Mixe teenagers of just eleven
and twelve years old. In 1994, our town, Ayutla, organised a movement resisting
the influence of political parties that were threatening – and still threaten – our
communal structures. ‘Ayutla defends its customs and traditions’, the walls of
the main streets read. The topic was in the air, in the talks given in Ayuujk by
our elders and in the assemblies we children frequently attended alongside the
adults. But Chiapas felt far away. Gradually, we came to understand that what was
happening there was relevant to us too.<br><br>

In the Mixe region, as in many Indigenous communities, which are always resisting
in some way or other, a land defence movement had been taking shape since the
end of the seventies. Thinkers from the sierra, such as Floriberto Díaz, held intense
debates and came up with their own categories to explain the workings of our
communities. Along with the Zapotecan anthropologist Jaime Martínez Luna,
Floriberto coined the word communality and described it as a political concept. In
this context, we were swept up by the EZLN’s tide, a tide that, happily, drowned
out Carlos Salinas de Gortari’s ‘Solidaridad’, sung by a Televisa chorus during his
1988 presidential candidacy and repeated endlessly on our screens. Despite the
mobilisation within our community and the long process begun in the Mixe
region and many other territories, we Indigenous peoples were largely absent from
mainstream media and national debates; it seemed we were only of interest to those
taking an indigenist perspective.<br><br>

Our reading and debates at school crystallised into pieces of writing in Spanish, our
second language, that tried to show solidarity with the Zapatistas. When I moved
to Mexico City, the relatives who took me in were quite involved in the movement.
I remember visiting the impressive Zócalo with my uncle Iván, who enthusiastically
explained many things to me the day of the March of the Colour of the Earth took
over the political heart of the country.<br><br>

Perhaps for many on the left now immersed in defending what the President
of the Republic has called the Fourth Transformation, the EZLN is no longer a
valid interlocutor; perhaps for the young people immersed in the ups and downs
of left-wing party politics, neo-Zapatismo is now a thing of the past. But one
thing is undeniable: there was a time when the Zapatista movement, the struggles
of Indigenous communities and debates about the architecture of the Mexican
state in relation to the possible fulfilment of the San Andrés Accords were front
and centre in the media and constituted a key national issue. Scandalised rightwing 
voices warned of a possible ‘balkanisation’ if Indigenous nations’ right to free
determination was recognised, columnists were sparking debates left, right and
centre based on contradictory stances, and the evolution of the Zapatista movement
hogged the headlines. After 1994, the way this country conceived of itself was
transformed forever. It wasn’t just about Indigenous communities; Zapatismo held
a mirror up to the foundational myths of the Mexican state. The narrative of a
single mestizo nation, the ideological aspects of Mexican identity as constructed
by those in power, the nationalism that extracted cultural and symbolic elements
from Indigenous peoples while doing everything possible to erase them, and the
very structure of the state itself were put under scrutiny. The images reflected
in that new mirror fractured ideological certainties built with such dedication,
especially by the governments of the Institutional Revolutionary Party (Partido
Revolucionario Institutional – PRI) following the 1910 Revolution. While the
EZLN resisted paramilitarism and the violence unleashed against it, society – if we
can speak of society in the singular – responded by questioning itself profoundly.
If we aren’t that mestizo nation in which Indigenous peoples are nothing but an
anthropological curiosity on the verge of disappearing, if we aren’t that country
soon to reach the developmentalist ideals of the first world, then who are we?
Perhaps the youngest of us now fighting for the rights of Indigenous peoples don’t
quite realise how profoundly our practices and discourses were permeated by that
uprising that began in a visible way thirty years ago. We wouldn’t be able to talk
about the things we talk about without everything we inherited from Zapatismo,
even if we didn’t participate directly in the movement that transformed ‘the spirit
of an era’. The EZLN created a new lexicon for realities and utopias and succeeded
in bringing topics previously disregarded into political debates. For many people,
being Indigenous became something they no longer had to deny, something that
spoke of a fight they could be proud of. Even those who did not or do not agree
with the Zapatista movement have been impacted by its practices and discourses.
Gattopardist (a principle according to which the appearance of things must change
in order for the structure to remain the same) though the strategy may have been,
the very architecture of the Mexican state was indeed transformed. It failed to fully
implement the San Andrés Accords it had signed, but the government did set in
motion a series of changes in many of its institutions.<br><br>

Without the Zapatista uprising, the National Institute of Indigenous Languages or
the General Organisation for Intercultural and Bilingual Education, to mention just
a couple of examples, would not have become realities. While attacking Zapatista
communities, state administrative structures launched a handful of projects,
departments, and initiatives that took into account the existence of Indigenous
peoples and whose purpose was to see to their needs.<br><br>

The EZLN’s powerful discursive influence remains alive. Even the discourses of
power in current pre-campaigns (let’s call them what they are) for the Presidency
of the Republic have appropriated their language: Claudia Sheinbaum says,
paraphrasing Comandanta Ramona: ‘Never again a Mexico without us women.’
López Obrador, for his part, often uses one of Zapatismo’s principles as a slogan,
that of ‘governing while obeying [the people]’, despite his followers discrediting
the EZLN as a political force and accusing them of being merely an ‘invention’ of
Carlos Salinas de Gortari.<br><br>

Despite what neo-Zapatismo has meant for the history of this country, part of the
Mexican left has broken with the EZLN. The recent mobilisation calling for an
end to the war against the Zapatista peoples met with little response from leftwing party politics.
Armed attacks on their key communities and Caracoles are
evidence that the violence against the movement and their support bases continues
or is worsening through incursions into their territories, though it rarely makes the
headlines, nor does the president, who once visited Zapatista territory, speak up in
his daily morning press conferences about Chiapas and the violence that envelops
it.<br><br>

Various people have explained how this distancing arose. Beyond the anecdotes
and accusations, I believe that key to this was a fundamental ideological shift that
the EZLN figured out how to make but a large part of the (now Obradorista)
left could not. After the Mexican government reneged on the previously signed
San Andrés Accords, the EZLN and its bases created self-governing organisational
structures called Caracoles, represented by Good Governance Committees (Juntas
de Buen Gobierno). The fact that these worked and continue to work outside the
logic of the state was evidence of how far we were by now from the EZLN that, in
its First Declaration of the Lacandon Jungle, called for the fight to go on until ‘a
government of our free and democratic country’ was formed. This was no longer
about taking power so that the rights of Indigenous peoples would be guaranteed
via the scaffolding of the state and a new government would be formed; now
the idea was to build a different reality through self-governmental mechanisms.
The EZLN, which, when it began, emphasised that its struggle was linked to
the Mexican Constitution, now distanced itself from enthusiasm for reforming
the state and fought instead for the creation of concrete structures to coordinate
communal life based on anti-capitalist principles. This important shift reminds
me too of the Kurdistan Workers’ Party’s change in objectives, which went from 
fighting for its own state to the creation of confederate, self-generated, autonomous
bodies, a goal that transcends the model of the nation-state; we might call it a
‘post-state’ model.<br><br>

A large proportion of the Mexican left finds it difficult to understand and decipher
movements that don’t involve conquering state institutions; it was to be expected
that many people found the EZLN’s new vision incomprehensible. The K’iche’
anthropologist Gladys Tzul Tzul speaks of the ‘desire for the state’, and it is precisely
that desire Zapatismo turned away from. Those who continue to see the taking
of state power as the only goal of political struggle are more comfortable with
Obradorismo.<br><br>

This profound misunderstanding is reflected in various phenomena. At the
beginning of this administration, both Adelfo Regino, current director of the
National Institute of Indigenous Peoples and ex-Zapatista mentor, and López
Obrador himself promised that an Indigenous reform would finally make the San
Andrés Accords a reality, though the EZLN has clearly said that their members
have moved on from those reformist agreements. Another piece of evidence that
the party-political left does not fully understand Zapatismo’s current objectives is
the irritation and concern its members expressed when the EZLN failed to join
Obradorismo – natural allies, as they saw it, based on their shared ‘desire for the
state’. A third piece of evidence was the insistence with which they referred to
María de Jesús Patricio Martínez as ‘Presidential candidate’, despite her being the
spokesperson for a broad movement that had no pretensions to state power but
rather sought to bring topics into the public debate that none of the candidates
would touch.<br><br>

Who feels that the EZLN speaks for them today? Not, it would seem, the
Mexican politicians who appropriate their phrases but not their principles, nor
their detractors, who criticise the EZLN for not having joined Obradorismo, nor
even those who long for the fulfilment of the San Andrés Accords. Who does
the EZLN speak to today? It speaks to those who question the developmentalist
model that has brought humanity face to face with climate emergency, the greatest
catastrophe yet provoked by capitalism. If the world’s liberal democracies and the
nation-state have worked to capitalism’s advantage – a capitalism that is furnishing
us with death – then the EZLN offers us urgent, radical strategies. Zapatismo no
longer talks about ‘advancing towards the capital to defeat the Mexican Federal
Army’ and subsequently forming a new government. But it does still offer to create
a world that encompasses many worlds, a world that is certainly not achieved by
taking state power in order to reform it. We must follow the EZLN in this shift, if
we are to make life possible in a future that seems to promise us only death.<br><br><br><br><br>

*Yásnaya Elena A. Gil (trans. Ellen Jones)*
